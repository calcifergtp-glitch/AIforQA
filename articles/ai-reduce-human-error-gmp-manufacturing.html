Human Error in GMP Manufacturing: The Persistent Risk

Human error can contribute to deviations, documentation gaps, and other problems in pharmaceutical manufacturing, but it should not be treated as a root cause until an investigation supports that conclusion. CGMP requirements address qualified personnel and training (21 CFR 211.25), written production procedures and contemporaneous documentation (21 CFR 211.100), and quality-unit review and investigation (21 CFR 211.192). These rules apply whether a manufacturer uses AI or not.

An investigation should look beyond the label “human error.” Depending on the event, relevant factors may include procedure design, equipment condition, workload, task interfaces, training, materials, environment, and supervision. These are questions to examine, not causes to assume. Avoiding blame before the evidence is gathered helps keep the investigation focused on what happened and what controls may need improvement.

AI may help organize records, flag a possible omission, or make a procedure easier to check. These are possible uses, not proven error-reduction results. A site should define the tool’s intended use, test it with representative records, set human-review and escalation rules, and measure performance before relying on it in a GMP workflow.

Understanding Where Human Errors Occur in GMP Manufacturing

Before evaluating a tool, identify the specific task or record problem the site wants to understand. The examples below are possible error modes to examine; they are not a claim that every site experiences them or that AI can reliably prevent them.

Procedure deviations can include a missed step, an out-of-sequence action, or use of the wrong material or equipment. For covered production and process-control functions, 21 CFR 211.100 requires written procedures to be followed and documented at the time of performance; any deviation must be recorded and justified. The site’s approved procedures determine how an event is reported and assessed.

Data-entry and transcription errors can occur when measurements, weights, calculations, or identifiers are recorded or transferred. Electronic systems can reduce some manual steps, but they still need controls appropriate to their use. FDA’s data-integrity guidance explains the agency’s current thinking; it is guidance, while the applicable CGMP regulations establish the requirements.

Material and label mix-up errors are among the most consequential in pharmaceutical manufacturing. U.S. CGMP requires that containers of drugs, components, and materials be properly identified at all stages. Despite labeling controls, mix-ups remain a reported failure mode in FDA enforcement actions and warning letters.

Calculation and verification errors occur during yield calculations, environmental monitoring limit assessments, in-process control verifications, and similar numerical tasks. Errors that are not caught through second-person verification may propagate into batch records and ultimately affect disposition decisions.

Omitted steps may be difficult to detect until a record or process review reveals a gap. A digital prompt can help surface a missing field, but it cannot prove that a physical activity was performed correctly. The controlled procedure and any required verification remain in effect.

AI Uses That May Be Worth Evaluating

Some software can flag missing fields, compare entries with configured rules, or help search historical quality records. Whether an AI feature adds value depends on its intended use, the underlying system, the data, and its tested performance. The examples below are possibilities to assess, not proven results.

Electronic Batch Record Monitoring and Step Completion Verification

In an electronic batch record system, configured checks may flag a missing field, an unexpected value, or a step that does not match the approved workflow. A site evaluating an AI feature should determine whether it identifies these cases accurately, points reviewers to the source information, and handles uncertainty safely. A flag does not show that a physical task was completed correctly.

A prompt may let an operator or reviewer catch an incomplete field before record review. It does not authorize changing a procedure or skipping the site’s reporting process. If work departs from a written procedure, 21 CFR 211.100(b) requires the deviation to be recorded and justified; early detection does not remove that requirement.

Computer Vision for Label Verification and Material Identification

A site may evaluate whether a vision or scanning system can help check material labels or identifiers at a defined step. Performance can depend on packaging, lighting, camera position, label condition, and the approved workflow. Any alert or stop function should be assessed for false alarms, missed mismatches, and a safe fallback.

For a proposed vision system, first define what it is intended to detect and how staff will respond to uncertain or incorrect alerts. Computerized equipment used in CGMP activities is subject to applicable controls under 21 CFR 211.68. Electronic records and signatures may also be subject to Part 11 depending on how they are used. The site should assess the actual system and intended use instead of assuming one validation approach fits every tool.

Predictive Error Risk Scoring Based on Historical Patterns

A site could test whether historical event and record data can help identify process conditions associated with more frequent documentation or procedural issues. Any score should be treated as an unproven signal until tested on representative data, and it should not be used to rank or penalize individual employees. A qualified reviewer should consider context and decide whether additional checks are appropriate.

Historical data may be incomplete, inconsistently coded, or affected by past process changes. Any signal should be checked on separate representative data and reviewed for unintended effects. Do not use an unproven score to rank or penalize individual employees or to change oversight without a documented assessment. ICH Q9(R1) provides risk-management guidance; it does not establish that such a model will predict errors.

Automated Second-Person Verification Support

Some approved procedures require an independent verification for specific steps. A tool may display a checklist based on the current approved procedure, but a human verifier must perform the required check and document it using the site’s process. The checklist itself does not prove that verification occurred.

A structured checklist may make the expected review scope easier to see. Whether it improves verification quality must be demonstrated; it should not be described as reducing “rubber stamp” reviews without evidence.

Natural Language Processing for Deviation and OOS Pattern Detection

Text-search or summarization tools may help staff find related deviation or CAPA records. Search results can omit relevant material or group unlike events together, so reviewers should verify each source and determine whether a pattern is supported by the underlying evidence.

For U.S. drug products, periodic product-quality evaluation is addressed in 21 CFR 211.180(e). Trend analysis may support that review, but it does not by itself establish a cause or prove that an AI tool reduced error rates.

Regulatory Alignment: AI for Error Reduction in the GMP Framework

Deploying AI to reduce human error in GMP manufacturing must align with existing regulatory expectations for process controls, validation, and change management. Several key frameworks apply.

Process monitoring and control

FDA’s PAT framework describes a voluntary framework for innovative pharmaceutical development, manufacturing, and quality assurance. It does not specifically endorse AI for monitoring operator actions. Any proposed AI control should have a clearly defined use and evidence that it works as intended.

Computer System Validation Requirements

Computerized systems used in CGMP activities need controls appropriate to their function and intended use, including the applicable requirements in 21 CFR 211.68. The site should define what the AI is allowed to do, assess its impact, and document evidence that it performs as intended before relying on it in a controlled workflow. This article does not establish a single validation method for every AI tool.

Data Integrity and Audit Trail Requirements

When outputs enter regulated records, apply the record controls relevant to the system and use. FDA’s data-integrity guidance discusses completeness, consistency, and accuracy; computerized systems also have applicable controls under 21 CFR 211.68. Define how AI-generated material is reviewed, attributed, retained, and corrected in the site’s approved procedures.

Change Control for AI Model Updates

A model or vendor update can change how a tool behaves. The site should assess changes under its applicable procedures and controls, based on the system’s intended use and possible effect on product quality. ICH Q10 describes change-management principles for a pharmaceutical quality system; it is guidance and does not prescribe one AI-specific process.

Implementation Considerations and Realistic Expectations

Organizations considering AI for human error reduction should approach deployment with a realistic scope and a phased implementation strategy.

Begin with a clearly defined task and evaluate the tool on representative examples, including ordinary cases, known errors, and cases where the right result is “uncertain.” Record missed flags and false alerts. If a controlled pilot is justified, keep the output within the approved scope, assign a qualified reviewer, define escalation and fallback steps, and set acceptance criteria before starting. Consider expanding use only after documented results support it and the site has assessed any added control or decision-making function.

Throughout all phases, QA must maintain clear written procedures that define: what the AI is allowed to do, when human override applies, how AI outputs become (or do not become) part of the controlled GMP record, and how model performance is monitored over time.

Key Metrics for Measuring AI Impact on Human Error Rates

Demonstrating the effectiveness of AI error-reduction tools requires defining measurable outcomes before deployment and tracking them systematically.

Useful metrics include: rate of batch record corrections per batch (pre- vs. post-AI), rate of second-person verification deficiencies detected during QA review, frequency of procedural deviation reports attributed to human error root causes, OOS investigation rate and proportion attributed to manufacturing process steps versus analytical errors, and cycle time from deviation detection to closure (as a proxy for error complexity and investigation burden).

These measures can be reviewed with the site’s quality metrics. Define the baseline and counting rules in advance, and interpret changes alongside product, process, and investigation context; a change after implementation does not by itself prove the tool caused the change.

Summary: A Practical Path Forward

AI may help with a specific record-review or information-search task, but it should not be assumed to reduce human error. Define the intended use, evaluate performance, and keep decisions about procedures and product quality within the site’s approved quality processes.

Potential starting points include advisory checks for missing information or procedure steps and support for reviewing large sets of quality records. A site should select a limited use case, test it against its existing process, and expand only if documented results and controls support doing so.
