What Happens When AI Makes a GMP Mistake?

Compliance
2026-05-31 • 4 min read • Reviewed November 26, 2026
AI Governance GMP Compliance Risk Assessment CAPA Accountability

AI systems used in quality work can produce incorrect summaries, miss relevant records, or suggest actions that are not supported by the evidence. The impact depends on what the tool was allowed to do, how its output was reviewed, and whether a record, process, or product decision was affected. An incorrect AI output is not automatically a regulatory violation; the site must assess the event under its quality system and applicable requirements.

Examples of AI-Related Errors

An AI tool might summarize an investigation but omit a relevant result, cite a requirement that does not apply, or suggest a cause without enough evidence. A reviewer might also misunderstand the model’s limits or copy an output into a controlled record without checking it. These examples are hypothetical. They illustrate why intended use, source verification, and human review matter.

Quality-Unit Responsibilities Still Apply

Current good manufacturing practice requirements do not transfer responsibility to an algorithm. Under 21 CFR 211.22, the Quality Control Unit has authority over specified materials, products, procedures, and production-record review. 21 CFR 211.100 requires written production and process-control procedures to be followed and deviations to be recorded and justified. Under 21 CFR 211.192, production and control records are reviewed before batch release, and unexplained discrepancies or specification failures are investigated and documented.

These provisions are not AI-specific. Whether an AI-related event represents a compliance failure depends on the facts: the tool’s use, the affected procedure or record, the decisions made, and the site’s response.

A Practical Response When an AI Output May Be Wrong

The exact response should follow the site’s approved procedures. The steps below are a practical checklist, not a substitute for those procedures or a claim that every step is separately required by regulation.

  1. Stop relying on the questionable output. Use the established process and qualified reviewer while the issue is assessed.
  2. Assess possible impact. Through the site’s quality process, determine whether records, investigations, equipment, batches, or prior decisions may be affected. Do not rely on the model itself to decide whether a batch should be released or held.
  3. Preserve useful evidence. As recommended investigation practice, retain the relevant source records, model or service version, prompt or inputs, output, timestamps, user actions, and review decisions where available and permitted by the site’s procedures.
  4. Investigate and document. Record what happened, the scope checked, conclusions, and follow-up using the applicable procedures. Section 211.192 specifically requires written investigation records, conclusions, and follow-up for the discrepancies and failures it covers.
  5. Correct the cause and check effectiveness. Consider whether the issue came from data, model behavior, configuration, procedure design, training, vendor information, or human use. Route any corrective action, system change, or model update through the site’s approved quality and change-control processes.

Hypothetical Example: An Unsupported CAPA Suggestion

Imagine an AI tool drafts a CAPA after a pressure alarm and recommends changing a sterilization procedure. Before the proposal is approved or used, a reviewer checks the cited batch history and finds that the “precedent” does not exist. The reviewer stops use of that draft and follows the site’s procedures to document and assess the event. The team can then determine whether the tool’s sources, instructions, or review controls need correction. This example does not describe a real company or event.

Preventing Repeat Problems

Before introducing an AI tool into a GMP workflow, define its intended use, prohibited uses, source-data limits, review responsibility, and escalation path. Evaluate it with representative examples, including cases where the correct answer is “not enough information.” Keep a qualified person responsible for decisions that affect controlled records or product quality. Monitor errors and near misses, and reassess the tool after changes to the model, data, configuration, or workflow.

Risk-management guidance such as ICH Q9(R1) can support a structured assessment. The site should distinguish binding requirements from guidance and from its own recommended controls.

Summary

An AI error can range from a harmless draft mistake to an event that affects a controlled record or quality decision. The response depends on the facts and the site’s approved procedures. Preserve relevant evidence, assess impact through the Quality Unit, investigate where required, and improve controls based on what the evidence shows. Avoid calling every model error a regulatory violation or assuming human review alone makes a system safe.
