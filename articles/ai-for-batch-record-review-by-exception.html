AI for Batch Record Review by Exception

GxP & Validation
Published 2026-05-31 • Reviewed 2026-11-26 • 6 min read
Batch Record Review GMP Data Integrity Audit Trail Review Quality Management System Manufacturing

Batch records help show what happened while a medicine was made. AI can help find missing entries, unusual values, or records that may need closer attention. But an alert is only a lead for review. It does not prove there is a problem, and it cannot approve or release a batch.

In plain words: AI can point to a page or entry worth checking. The Quality Unit still reviews the records and makes the batch decision.

What “review by exception” means

Review by exception means using defined rules or checks to bring unusual, missing, or out-of-range information to a reviewer’s attention. Some electronic batch record systems can already flag simple conditions, such as a required field left blank or a process step completed out of sequence. AI may add help with text searches, summaries, or patterns across multiple records.

Exception flags can help organize work. They do not change the underlying records or erase the need to review the complete information required by the site’s procedures and applicable regulations. A “no alert” result is not proof that a batch is acceptable.

What U.S. rules say

For drug products subject to 21 CFR Part 211, section 211.188 describes batch production and control records. Section 211.192 requires production and control records, including packaging and labeling records, to be reviewed and approved by the Quality Control Unit before a batch is released or distributed. It also requires unexplained discrepancies and failures to meet specifications to be thoroughly investigated, whether or not the batch has already been distributed.

These rules assign responsibilities to the Quality Unit. They do not approve a particular AI tool, make an AI alert a required batch record, or let software make the final release decision. FDA’s data-integrity guidance says firms should use meaningful, risk-based strategies to ensure data are reliable and accurate. The details depend on the actual process and system.

In the European Union or another jurisdiction, assess the applicable GMP and computerized-system requirements separately. Do not assume one country’s rules apply everywhere.

Where AI may assist

  • Find missing information: Point to required fields, attachments, signatures, or linked records that appear absent.
  • Compare records: Help check batch identifiers, material lots, equipment IDs, and timestamps across approved systems.
  • Surface unusual values: Flag values or sequences that differ from defined limits or an approved comparison method.
  • Search and summarize: Prepare a source-linked summary of exceptions, related deviations, and open actions for a reviewer to verify.
  • Group similar items: Suggest that several entries may share a theme, while leaving the reviewer to confirm whether they are truly related.

A flag should state what it found, where it found it, which data it used, and any limits or missing information. The reviewer should be able to open the original record. If the output cannot be traced to its source, treat it as unverified.

Example: a signal that needs checking

Illustrative example only; this is not a real company or reported result. During a review, an AI tool highlights that one batch had more equipment pauses than recent comparable batches. It links each pause to a timestamp and equipment log. A QA reviewer checks the logs, the approved batch record, and any related deviation. The pauses turn out to have different causes, and the reviewer follows the existing procedure to document the assessment. The AI identified a pattern to examine; it did not determine product impact or batch disposition.

A controlled review process

  1. Define the task. State exactly what the tool should flag or summarize. For example: “List missing attachments required by this approved checklist.”
  2. Confirm the data. Check that the records belong to the correct product, batch, equipment, and time period. Identify missing or delayed data before interpreting an alert.
  3. Review the source. A qualified person opens the original record and checks whether the AI summary is accurate and complete.
  4. Follow approved procedures. Investigate discrepancies and assess product impact using the existing quality system. The authorized Quality Unit makes and documents the batch decision.
  5. Keep the trail. Retain the relevant output, source references, corrections, system or model version, reviewer, and rationale as required by the applicable procedure and recordkeeping rules.

Controls to consider before use

First write down the intended use: what the tool does, which records it reads, and how its output may affect the review. Then assess the risk of a wrong, missed, incomplete, or late flag. Controls should match the effect a mistake could have on product quality and patient safety.

  • Test with approved, representative records, including known missing entries, discrepancies, and unusual cases.
  • Check both missed flags and unnecessary flags. Too many alerts can cause reviewers to overlook the important ones.
  • Verify that the system links the right batch, product, equipment, and source record.
  • Set access limits and protect confidential records. Use only services approved for the information involved.
  • Document who reviews outputs and how corrections, overrides, and escalation are handled.
  • Control changes to the model, prompts, data connections, and workflow. Reassess whether the tool still works for its intended use after a meaningful change.
  • Assess applicable computerized-system, electronic-record, signature, and validation requirements for the actual use. Do not assume the same requirements apply to every AI tool.

EMA’s 2024 reflection paper on AI in the medicinal-product lifecycle discusses risk-based development and use, data quality, and the context in which a system is used. It is a reflection paper, not a product-specific approval or permission to automate batch release.

Common mistakes

  • Treating “no alert” as “no issue”: The tool may miss a problem or lack access to a needed record.
  • Letting a score decide: An anomaly score does not establish a deviation, product impact, or release status.
  • Ignoring source records: Fluent summaries can still be wrong or omit context.
  • Making exception rules too narrow: A rule catches only what it was designed to catch. Keep required review and investigation procedures active.
  • Assuming every AI use has the same compliance impact: Assess the real intended use, records, workflow, and decision influence.

A sensible first use

Start with a support task that helps reviewers locate information, such as checking for missing attachments or preparing a source-linked exception list. Keep the existing review and release process in place while people compare the tool’s output with the underlying records. Expand only when the organization has documented the intended use, risks, review steps, and required system controls.

Key takeaway

AI can make it easier to find information that deserves attention during batch review. It cannot turn a missing record into a complete one, prove that a batch is acceptable, or take responsibility away from the Quality Unit. Use alerts to guide a careful review of traceable source records, and make the batch decision through approved procedures.
