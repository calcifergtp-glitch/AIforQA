Assessing AI Service Providers for GMP Use: Questions for Pharmaceutical QA

AI Governance
Published November 27, 2026•7 min read
AI GovernanceRisk AssessmentGMPComplianceData Integrity

A pharmaceutical company remains responsible for the quality of work it outsources. When an AI service may support a GMP activity, QA needs to understand what it does, what information it handles, and how the provider manages changes and problems.

In plain words: Before relying on an AI company, learn what it will do, what happens to your information, and how you will know when the service changes.

Start with the exact job

Describe the intended use in plain language: users, process, records, decisions, and expected benefit. “Summarize a public regulation for staff training” differs from “recommend whether a batch record is acceptable.” A broad label like “AI assistant” is not enough to set controls.

Write down what the tool may do, what it must never do, and who checks the output. Assess separate tasks separately when their data or impact differs.

What guidance says about outsourced work

ICH Q10 discusses assessing a provider before outsourcing, defining responsibilities in writing, monitoring performance, and reviewing improvements. Controls should fit the activity and applicable requirements.

FDA’s data-integrity guidance discusses controls for data created, changed, stored, or processed in regulated work. For computerized systems, 21 CFR 211.68 addresses electronic equipment controls; 21 CFR 211.22 describes the quality control unit’s responsibilities. This does not mean every AI website automatically falls under every electronic-record requirement. Determine whether the specific use and records bring relevant requirements into scope.

Questions to ask the provider

  • What does it do? Request features, limits, supported inputs, and known failure modes.
  • Where does information go? Ask whether prompts and files are retained, used to train models, shared with subcontractors, or transferred across regions.
  • How are access and records controlled? Ask about security, audit evidence, retention, export, and deletion.
  • How are changes handled? Ask how model and software changes are tested, communicated, and rolled back.
  • How are problems reported? Ask about outages, security events, incorrect output, support, and notification times.
  • Can your team review the service? Ask what objective evidence is available and how performance can be assessed.
  • Who is responsible? Put responsibilities, communications, support, and records in an appropriate written agreement.

Keep your own controls

Provider documentation informs your assessment; it does not replace it. Decide what information staff may enter, whether output could affect a regulated record or decision, what human review is required, and how the use will be documented. Check important outputs against trustworthy sources and train users to recognize uncertainty.

For higher-impact uses, define acceptance criteria and testing that reflect the real workflow. Monitor performance and assess provider updates. If the provider cannot give enough information for your organization to control the intended use, marketing claims are not evidence.

Warning signs

  • The provider cannot explain what data it keeps or how it uses it.
  • Important changes happen without notice or a way to assess impact.
  • There is no useful path to report incidents or get support.
  • “Compliant” or “validated” sales claims have no evidence tied to your intended use.
  • Your team cannot export needed records or plan for outages.

A practical first step

Make a one-page scoping record: intended use, information entered, possible effect of a wrong answer, human reviewer, record impact, provider contacts, and open questions. Decide whether the idea should stop, be tested outside GMP, or move into formal quality and validation review.

Key point: Choose and oversee an AI provider based on the job, information, and controls you can verify.
